Baby sleep schedules by age

Every age has its own rhythm. These summaries use total sleep ranges consistent with the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommendations endorsed by the American Academy of Pediatrics, with wake windows from standard pediatric sleep guidance. Use the builder above to turn any of them into clock times for your baby's wake-up.

0-1 months: sleep in short rounds

Wake windows of 45-90 minutes and 4-5 naps a day, with 14-17 hours of total sleep. There is no real clock schedule yet. Watch the clock loosely and the baby closely, and expect a late bedtime around 9:00-10:00 PM.

2-3 months: the day starts to take shape

Wake windows stretch to about 1.5-2 hours, with 4 naps plus a short late-day catnap for many babies. Total sleep is about 14-16 hours. A steadier wake-up time in the morning helps the whole day line up.

4-5 months: three naps and an earlier bedtime

Most babies settle into 3 naps with wake windows of 1.5-2.5 hours and 12-15 hours of total sleep. Bedtime often moves earlier, landing between 6:30 and 7:30 PM on a 7:00 AM wake-up.

6-8 months: three naps, then two

Wake windows of 2-3 hours and 12-15 hours of total sleep. Many babies drop the third catnap in this stretch. On 2-nap days, stretch each wake window a little and pull bedtime earlier to cover the gap.

9-11 months: two solid naps

Two naps of about 1-1.5 hours each, wake windows of 3-4 hours, and 12-15 hours of total sleep. The last wake window of the day is the longest, usually close to 4 hours before bedtime.

12-14 months: two naps, one on the way out

Wake windows of 3-4.5 hours and 11-14 hours of total sleep. Some babies still need 2 naps while others start refusing the second one. Shorter naps and a later bedtime for a week or two are the usual signs the 2-to-1 transition has begun.

15-17 months: one midday nap

One nap of about 2 hours after roughly 5.5 hours awake, then about 5 hours awake before bed. Total sleep is 11-14 hours. Lunch before the nap helps a tired toddler hold out until nap time.

18-24 months: one nap, steady routine

One nap of 1.5-2 hours, usually after about 6 hours awake, with 4.5-5 hours awake before a 7:00-8:00 PM bedtime. Total sleep is 11-14 hours. At this age the same nap start every day does more for bedtime than any single trick.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good sleep schedule for a baby?
A good schedule matches your baby's age: newborns nap often with wake windows of 45-90 minutes, babies 4-5 months old usually take 3 naps, and most toddlers settle into one midday nap by 15-18 months. Total sleep matters most: about 14-17 hours for newborns, 12-15 hours at 4-11 months, and 11-14 hours at 1-2 years.
How many naps does my baby need?
It depends on age. Newborns take 4-5 short naps, babies around 4-5 months usually take 3, most babies drop to 2 naps between 6 and 9 months, and one nap is typical from about 15 months. Watch your baby's mood in the last wake window of the day: a baby who falls apart before bedtime may still need the extra nap.
What are wake windows and why do they matter?
A wake window is the stretch of awake time between sleeps. Babies can only stay awake happily for so long by age: about 1 hour for a newborn, around 2 hours at 4 months, 3 hours at 9 months, and 5-6 hours for a toddler on one nap. Putting a baby down near the end of their wake window usually means an easier fall asleep and a longer nap.
What time should my baby go to bed?
Bedtime follows the last wake window, so it moves with age and with the day's last nap. Newborns often go down late, around 9:00-10:00 PM. From about 4 months, a bedtime between 6:30 and 8:00 PM works for most babies, and toddlers on one nap usually land between 7:00 and 8:00 PM. An earlier bedtime is the fix on days when naps run short.
What if my baby will not follow the schedule?
That is normal. This is a sample schedule, not a rule. Shift everything by 15-30 minutes to fit your baby, keep wake-up time steady for a few days, and follow sleepy cues over the clock. If sleep stays difficult or you are worried about breathing, feeding or growth, ask your pediatrician.
