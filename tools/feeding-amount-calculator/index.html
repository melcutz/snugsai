How Much Formula Should My Baby Eat?

A feeding amount calculator for formula and expressed breastmilk, by weight and age.

Enter your baby's weight, age and what is in the bottle, and this calculator works out a typical total per day and a per feed amount. It uses the standard rule of thumb of about 2.5 oz of formula per pound of body weight per day, and the well studied average breastmilk intake of about 25 oz per day, so you can size bottles with confidence instead of guessing.

How much should a baby eat, by age?

Amounts climb quickly in the first two months, then level off. A bigger baby does not keep needing more and more milk: once the daily total reaches roughly 24 to 32 oz, it stays in that range until solids start to share the load.

0 to 1 month

Newborns start small and ramp up fast. In the first days a feed may be just 1 to 2 oz (30 to 60 mL), growing to about 2 to 3 oz (60 to 90 mL) per feed by the end of the first month. Expect 8 to 12 feeds a day, roughly every 2 to 3 hours, for a daily total that builds toward about 18 to 24 oz (530 to 710 mL). The 2.5 oz per pound rule starts to fit once your baby is back to birth weight and feeding well.

1 to 2 months

Most babies take about 4 to 5 oz (120 to 150 mL) per feed, 6 to 8 times a day, for a daily total near 24 oz (710 mL). This is the age where the weight rule works best: a 10 lb baby at 2.5 oz per pound lands at 25 oz a day, right in the typical range.

2 to 4 months

Feeds get bigger and a little less frequent: about 4 to 6 oz (120 to 180 mL) per feed, 5 to 6 times a day, totaling 24 to 32 oz (710 to 950 mL) a day. The calculator caps the daily total at 32 oz, because intake plateaus here even as weight keeps climbing.

4 to 6 months

A typical feed is 6 to 8 oz (180 to 240 mL), 4 to 5 times a day, still totaling about 24 to 32 oz (710 to 950 mL) a day. If your baby is draining every bottle and still seems hungry, talk to your pediatrician about whether it is time to start solids, which many babies begin around 6 months.

6 to 12 months

Milk stays the main source of nutrition through the first year, at roughly 24 oz (710 mL) a day for most babies, while solids gradually take a bigger share. The weight rule no longer applies at this age, so treat the calculator result as a rough guide and follow your pediatrician's plan.

A note on expressed breastmilk

Breastmilk intake follows a different pattern. Research puts the average at about 25 oz (750 mL) per day from 1 to 6 months, with a typical range of 19 to 30 oz (570 to 900 mL), and it barely rises after the first month because the milk's composition changes to match your baby. That is why a breastfed baby's bottle often stays around 3 to 4 oz even as they grow, while a formula fed baby's bottle keeps getting bigger.

Ranges follow general guidance published by the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) and the CDC: about 2.5 oz of formula per pound of body weight per day in the early months, up to about 32 oz a day, and an average breastmilk intake near 25 oz a day between 1 and 6 months.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate how much formula my baby needs?
A widely used rule of thumb is about 2.5 oz (75 mL) of formula per pound of body weight per day in the first months, up to a cap of about 32 oz (960 mL) per day. Divide the daily total by the number of feeds to get a per feed amount. A 10 lb baby, for example, lands near 25 oz per day, or about 3 to 4 oz across 7 feeds.
How much expressed breastmilk should I put in a bottle?
Breastfed babies take in a fairly steady average of about 25 oz (750 mL) per day between 1 and 6 months, with a typical range of 19 to 30 oz. Divide that by the number of feeds: for 8 feeds, a bottle of about 3 oz is a common starting point. Unlike formula, the amount per feed stays fairly steady after the first month because the milk itself changes to match your baby.
Can a baby drink too much formula?
It can happen, because a bottle flows even when a baby is mostly comfort sucking. Watch for fullness cues such as turning away, sealing the lips, slowing down or pushing the bottle out, and try paced bottle feeding with pauses. Frequent large spit ups, fussiness after feeds or very rapid weight gain are worth raising with your pediatrician.
Why does my baby drink less than the calculator says?
The calculator gives a typical range, and healthy babies land above and below it. Appetite dips with teething, minor illness and distractions, and jumps during growth spurts. Hunger and fullness cues, steady weight gain and plenty of wet and dirty diapers matter more than hitting an exact number. If intake drops sharply or weight gain stalls, call your pediatrician.
Is this medical advice?
No. This calculator shares general feeding guidance published by sources such as the American Academy of Pediatrics and the CDC for planning purposes only. It is not medical advice. Babies who were premature, have reflux or have any medical condition may need a different plan, so follow your pediatrician's guidance for your baby.
