    Sleep regressions by age: signs, causes and how long each lasts

    Every window below follows the same pattern: a developmental leap lands, sleep wobbles while your baby's brain and body work on it, then things settle. The ages are approximate. Some babies sail through a window, others hit it a few weeks early or late.

    The 4 month sleep regression (around 3 to 5 months)

    What is driving it: this is the famous one, and it is not really a regression at all. Around 4 months your baby's sleep permanently matures into adult-like cycles with light and deep stages. Between cycles they now surface into light sleep, and if they do not yet know how to link cycles on their own, they wake fully and call for whatever helped them fall asleep.

    Signs: waking every 1 to 2 hours at night after previously sleeping longer stretches, naps shrinking to one 30 to 45 minute cycle, fussiness at bedtime, and a baby who is suddenly more alert and distractible during feeds.

    How long it lasts: the disrupted stretch usually runs 2 to 6 weeks. The new sleep pattern itself is permanent, which is why this window is really about building sustainable sleep habits rather than waiting for the old sleep to come back.

    What helps: keep the bedtime routine short and identical every night, put your baby down drowsy but awake for at least the first sleep of the night, make sure daytime feeds are full and unhurried, and pause for a minute or two before responding to a stir, since some wake-ups resettle on their own.

    The 6 month sleep regression (around 5 to 7 months)

    What is driving it: a cluster of smaller leaps rather than one big one. Rolling both ways, pushing up, early sitting, starting solids, first teeth, and a growing awareness that you exist even when out of sight. Any one of these can fragment a few nights; together they can fragment a few weeks.

    Signs: rolling in the crib and getting stuck or frustrated, a night waking or two returning after a good stretch, naps getting harder to start, and early morning wake-ups creeping in.

    How long it lasts: usually 1 to 3 weeks, and often milder than the 4 month change.

    What helps: give rolling and sitting practice lots of floor time in the day so the crib is less tempting as a practice studio, keep night responses calm, brief and boring, and check the schedule: many babies this age are getting ready to stretch wake windows, and an undertired baby fights sleep just as hard as an overtired one.

    The 8 to 10 month sleep regression

    What is driving it: the biggest motor leap since newborn days. Crawling, pulling to stand, cruising along furniture, plus separation anxiety reaching a peak and the drop from three naps to two. Babies this age also start understanding cause and effect: cry, and a favorite person appears. It is a lot, all at once.

    Signs: standing in the crib and crying because getting down is still a work in progress, strong protests when you leave the room, refusing the third nap or fighting the second, and night wakings that need you specifically.

    How long it lasts: typically 2 to 6 weeks, often the longest of the first year because several changes overlap.

    What helps: practice standing and, importantly, getting back down during the day; play short peekaboo and goodbye games to build the idea that you always come back; give a full minute before going in at night, since standing babies often lie down and resettle; and hold the two-nap schedule steady once you have made the switch, even on rough days.

    The 12 month sleep regression

    What is driving it: first steps and first words. Walking is so exciting that many toddlers would rather practice than sleep, and the new words and babbling get rehearsed at bedtime too. This is also the age where nap refusal makes parents suspect the second nap is done. Usually it is not, yet.

    Signs: refusing the morning or afternoon nap for days at a time, standing and chatting in the crib at bedtime, and a return of one night waking.

    How long it lasts: usually 1 to 3 weeks.

    What helps: keep offering both naps for at least two weeks before concluding anything; most 12 month olds still need two naps until somewhere between 13 and 18 months. Cap the morning nap if the afternoon one is the battle. Burn off the walking excitement with plenty of practice and outdoor time, and keep the bedtime routine predictable while your toddler tests it.

    The 18 month sleep regression

    What is driving it: independence. Your toddler has discovered "no", their own preferences, and that bedtime is negotiable if they protest hard enough. Add a language burst, a second round of separation anxiety, and canine teeth or molars arriving, and nights get loud for a while.

    Signs: full-scale bedtime battles, demanding extra stories, extra water, extra everything, nap refusal on some days, and early waking with strong opinions about it.

    How long it lasts: typically 2 to 6 weeks.

    What helps: offer small, real choices inside the routine (these pajamas or those, this book or that one) so the need for control gets met without moving bedtime; keep the single nap, or quiet time in the crib or room on days it is refused, because dropping the nap now usually makes nights worse; and hold boundaries kindly and identically every night, since this window is as much about testing as it is about sleep.

    The 24 month sleep regression (2 years)

    What is driving it: a two year old's brain is busy: a language explosion, an imagination switching on (which brings new fears of the dark and of monsters), second molars, and often a move to a big kid bed or a new sibling arriving in the same season. Nap resistance peaks here too, even though most two year olds still need the nap.

    Signs: stalling and new fears at bedtime, climbing out or calling out repeatedly, skipping the nap then crashing by dinner, and very early mornings.

    How long it lasts: typically 2 to 6 weeks.

    What helps: keep offering the nap or a genuine quiet time, since most two year olds still need daytime sleep even while protesting it; use a toddler clock or a simple picture routine chart so the rules come from the chart, not from you at 9 PM; add a nightlight or a comfort object if fears are new; and if a big bed move is planned, try not to land it in the middle of this window.

    When it is probably not a regression

    Plenty of rough patches get blamed on regressions when something simpler is going on. Before you settle in to wait one out, rule these out:

    • A schedule problem. Too much daytime sleep, a last wake window that is too long or too short, or a nap that needs dropping. Schedule issues do not fix themselves in two weeks, but they do fix quickly once you adjust the timing. Our wake window calculator and nap transition checker help you test this.
    • Illness or pain. Ear infections, colds, reflux and teething all wreck sleep and none of them are regressions. A sudden change in a baby who was sleeping well, especially with any symptom, deserves a health check first.
    • A recent disruption. Travel across time zones, a house move, starting daycare, or a new sibling can each cost one to two weeks of sleep without any developmental leap involved.
    • A sleep association. If your baby can only fall asleep being fed, rocked or held, they will need that same help at every natural night waking. That pattern does not resolve on a regression timeline, but it does respond to gradual changes in how they fall asleep.

    When to call the pediatrician

    Broken sleep is usually a phase. These are not. Call your pediatrician the same day if your baby:

    • Has a fever (any fever in a baby under 3 months is urgent; call right away)
    • Is feeding poorly, refusing feeds, or eating far less than usual
    • Has noticeably fewer wet diapers than normal
    • Cries inconsolably, or cries in a way that sounds like pain and is unusual for them
    • Seems to have trouble breathing, or snores loudly with pauses
    • Is unusually sleepy or hard to wake

    And trust your instincts generally: if something feels wrong beyond ordinary tiredness, a call is never the wrong move.

    Sleep regression questions, answered

    What ages do sleep regressions happen?
    The commonly reported windows are around 4 months, 6 months, 8 to 10 months, 12 months, 18 months and 24 months. Each one lines up with a developmental leap, such as a maturing sleep cycle at 4 months, separation anxiety and standing at 8 to 10 months, and independence and language growth at 18 months. Not every baby hits every window, and the exact week varies.
    How long does a sleep regression last?
    Most regressions ease within 1 to 6 weeks. Shorter ones, like the 6 month and 12 month windows, often settle in 1 to 3 weeks. The 4 month change is different: a baby's sleep cycles permanently become more adult-like, so what settles is a new pattern rather than a return to the old one. If disrupted sleep lasts much longer than 6 weeks, look for another cause, such as a schedule problem, illness or a sleep association, and talk to your pediatrician.
    Is a sleep regression a real medical thing?
    Sleep regression is a popular label parents and sleep consultants use, not a medical diagnosis. The developmental changes behind it are real: maturing sleep architecture around 4 months, new motor skills, separation anxiety and growing independence all genuinely disrupt sleep. The label is useful shorthand, but it should never replace checking for illness or asking your pediatrician when something feels off.
    What can I do to get through a sleep regression?
    Keep the bedtime routine and wake time steady, give your baby lots of daytime practice with the new skill they are working on, keep night responses calm and boring, and avoid starting new habits you do not want to keep. Protect the nap schedule for their age rather than dropping a nap in the middle of a regression. Most of all, trade off nights with a partner if you can: regressions end, and your job is mostly to stay consistent until they do.
    When should I call the pediatrician about broken sleep?
    Call your pediatrician if your baby has a fever, is feeding poorly or refusing feeds, has noticeably fewer wet diapers, cries inconsolably in a way that is unusual for them, seems in pain, has trouble breathing, or if you are ever worried. Trust your instincts: you know your baby's normal, and a sudden change with any of these signs is worth a call the same day.
