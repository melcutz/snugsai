Percentiles are calculated from the WHO Child Growth Standards (World Health Organization, 2006). A single reading is a snapshot. What matters most is the pattern of growth over time, which is exactly what your pediatrician follows at each check-up.

What a growth percentile means

A growth percentile places your baby in a line of 100 babies of the same age and sex, ordered by size. If your baby's weight is at the 30th percentile, about 30 of those babies weigh less and about 70 weigh more. The 50th percentile is the middle of the line. It is a position, not a score: there is no pass or fail, and a higher percentile is not better than a lower one.

The reference group behind this calculator comes from the WHO Child Growth Standards, a study of healthy, mostly breastfed babies raised in good conditions in six countries (Brazil, Ghana, India, Norway, Oman and the United States). Because the group describes how children grow when conditions are good, doctors treat it as a standard to compare against, not just an average of whoever happened to be measured.

What a percentile does not mean

  • It is not a grade. A baby at the 20th percentile is not doing worse than a baby at the 80th. Healthy babies settle at many different positions.
  • It is not a target. Your baby does not need to reach or stay at the 50th percentile. Their own steady curve matters far more than any single line.
  • Different measures, different percentiles. A baby can be at the 70th percentile for length and the 35th for weight. That mix is common and usually just reflects their build.
  • One reading is a snapshot. A single measurement can be thrown off by a full tummy, a wiggly baby on the scale, or a tape measure held loosely. The pattern across several check-ups is what counts.

If a percentile sits below the 3rd or above the 97th, or your baby's curve crosses two or more percentile lines between visits, mention it at your next pediatrician appointment. It is often still normal, and it is exactly the kind of pattern a pediatrician is trained to judge in person.

How this calculator works

For every age from birth to 24 months, the WHO publishes three numbers for each measurement and sex, known as the L, M and S values. M is the median, S describes the spread, and L adjusts for the natural skew in measurements like weight. Your baby's measurement is turned into a z-score with the standard LMS formula, and that z-score becomes a percentile. Ages between the published monthly values are interpolated, so a baby of 6 months and 2 weeks gets their own result rather than being rounded to a whole month.

For children under 2, the CDC and the American Academy of Pediatrics recommend plotting growth on these WHO standards rather than the older CDC charts. Source: WHO Child Growth Standards, World Health Organization.

Frequently asked questions

How do I use this growth percentile calculator?
Choose your baby's sex, set their age, pick metric or imperial units, and enter any measurements you have: weight, length and head circumference. Each percentile appears instantly. Leave a field blank if you do not have that measurement yet.
What does a growth percentile actually mean?
A percentile compares your baby with the WHO reference group of babies of the same age and sex. A weight at the 40th percentile means about 40 of 100 babies weigh less and about 60 weigh more. It is a position in the group, not a grade and not a percentage of some target weight.
Is the 50th percentile the goal?
No. The 50th percentile is simply the middle of the reference group. Healthy babies settle anywhere across a wide range, roughly the 3rd to the 97th percentile, and a baby tracking steadily along the 15th percentile is usually growing just as well as one tracking along the 85th.
Should I worry if my baby's percentile changes?
Small moves up or down are common, especially in the first months while babies find their own curve. What pediatricians watch is the pattern over several visits. If a percentile drops or climbs across two or more chart lines between visits, or you are ever worried about feeding or growth, bring it up with your pediatrician.
Is this medical advice?
No. This calculator is for general information only. It uses the WHO Child Growth Standards, the same reference the CDC and the American Academy of Pediatrics recommend for children under 2, but it cannot judge your baby's health. Always talk to your pediatrician about any concern.
