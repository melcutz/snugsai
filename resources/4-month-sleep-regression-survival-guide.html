The 4-Month Sleep Regression: A Survival Guide

Not medical advice. For emergencies, call your local emergency number.

Your baby was finally sleeping four-hour stretches. Then, almost overnight, they started waking every 45 to 90 minutes, naps fell apart, and you are wondering what broke. Nothing broke. Welcome to the 4-month sleep regression: the most famous, most dreaded, and most misunderstood phase of the first year.

What it actually is

Here is the reframe that changes everything: it is not really a regression. It is a progression. Between about 14 and 20 weeks, your baby's brain permanently rewires how sleep works. Newborns cycle between just two sleep stages and spend roughly half their sleep in deep, hard-to-disturb sleep. That is why young babies can sleep through a restaurant.

Around four months, the brain adds adult-like light sleep stages. Now each sleep cycle lasts 45 to 90 minutes and ends with a brief, partial waking. Adults do this too. We just roll over and forget it ever happened. Your baby's brain does a quick conditions check instead: is everything the same as when I fell asleep? If they fell asleep in your arms and woke up alone in the crib, or fell asleep nursing and the breast is gone, the mismatch triggers a full waking. And usually, crying.

This is why some babies sail through four months untouched while others suddenly wake all night long. The brain change happens to every baby. The disruption happens when the way they fall asleep cannot be recreated at 2 a.m.

Signs you are in it

  • Night wakings every 45 to 90 minutes after previously longer stretches
  • Naps suddenly shorten to 30 or 45 minutes, or get skipped entirely
  • Fussiness at bedtime and fighting sleep that used to come easily
  • Seeming hungrier at night (often comfort nursing rather than true hunger)

How long it lasts

The disruption typically lasts two to six weeks. But here is the part that trips parents up: the underlying change is permanent. Sleep does not go back to how it was before. It finds a new normal, and what that normal looks like depends on what you do during these weeks. That is actually good news, because it means your efforts now pay off for months.

What helps most

Two changes do most of the work. Everything else is support.

1. Fix the timing. Most four-month sleep falls apart because wake windows drift too long. At this age, most babies do well with 90 minutes to two hours awake, stretching longer as the day goes on. An overtired baby paradoxically fights sleep harder, not easier. Watching the clock instead of waiting for obvious tired signs fixes a surprising amount on its own.

2. Give them reps at falling asleep. This does not have to mean sleep training or crying it out. It can be as small as putting them down drowsy but awake for one nap a day, so falling asleep becomes a skill they can repeat at 2 a.m. without needing you to recreate the exact conditions.

More tactics that help

  • A boring, consistent bedtime routine. Same steps, same order, every night. The routine is a signal: sleep is coming.
  • A dark room and steady white noise. Light sleep stages are fragile. Darkness and continuous sound protect them.
  • A slightly earlier bedtime. During the regression, moving bedtime 15 to 30 minutes earlier often reduces night wakings, because it keeps overtiredness from compounding.
  • Treat false starts as a timing problem. Waking 30 to 45 minutes after bedtime, just as you sit down, is almost always the last wake window being slightly too long or too short. Adjust it by 15 minutes in either direction for three days and watch.

What does not help

  • Rice cereal in the bottle. It does not meaningfully improve sleep, and it is a choking risk before six months.
  • Switching strategies every three nights. Exhaustion makes everything feel urgent, but the families who struggle longest are usually the ones changing approach constantly. Pick something you can sustain while wrecked, and give it a full week.
  • Heavier blankets or sleep positioners. They do not fix cycle transitions and they work against safe sleep.

Safe sleep, even on the hardest nights

Back to sleep, on a firm flat surface, with nothing soft in the sleep space. These rules do not bend, even on the nights you would trade anything for three straight hours.

When to call your pediatrician

If disrupted sleep lasts more than a month with no improvement, if your baby snores or pauses breathing during sleep, or if you are not coping. You do not need to wait for a milestone to ask for help.

Frequently asked questions

Can I prevent the 4-month sleep regression?

No. The brain change happens on schedule regardless. But babies who can fall asleep without being fully soothed tend to sail through it with minimal disruption, which is why drowsy-but-awake practice beforehand helps so much.

Do I have to sleep train now?

No. Many pediatricians consider four months the earliest reasonable age for formal sleep training, but it is optional. Better timing and drowsy-but-awake practice help whether or not you ever sleep train.

Is it teething instead?

Teething disturbs a night or two around a tooth breaking through. Two or more weeks of broken sleep is the regression, or the schedule, not teeth.

Will sleep go back to normal afterward?

Not to the newborn kind of normal. It builds a new normal instead, and the habits you build during these weeks decide what that looks like for months to come.
