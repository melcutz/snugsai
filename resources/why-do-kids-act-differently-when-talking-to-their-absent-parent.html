Why Do Kids Act Differently When Talking to Their Absent Parent?

Not medical advice. For emergencies, call your local emergency number.

It’s common for children to experience complex emotions and exhibit confusing behaviors after a parental separation. When children express intense longing for an absent parent, it can be emotionally taxing for the parent they are currently with. The situation described by u/Competitive_Try_2511, where children express missing their dad intensely but then act resistant or uninterested during video calls, is a perplexing yet understandable phenomenon.

Understanding the Dynamics:

  • Emotional Regulation: Young children, especially those aged 2 and 4, are still developing their emotional regulation skills. They may not always know how to express their feelings coherently. Their strong desire to see their dad might be mixed with anxiety, guilt, or even disappointment if the reality of the separation or the father’s presence doesn’t perfectly match their ideal.
  • “Good Cop” vs. “Bad Cop” Behavior: Sometimes, children might feel pressure to portray their relationship with the absent parent in a positive light to the parent they are with. Conversely, when actually talking to the absent parent, the pressure might shift, or they might be struggling to engage due to the very emotions they’ve been expressing.
  • Projection and Guilt: The child saying “they don’t like me” or “I want to go to daddy’s house” could be a projection of their own confusion or guilt about having two separate lives and loving two different people. They might feel guilty enjoying their time with the parent they are with, leading to these statements.
  • Anticipation vs. Reality: The anticipation of seeing or talking to a parent they miss can build up to be immense. When the actual interaction occurs, it might not live up to the idealized expectation, leading to distraction or a feeling of being overwhelmed. This can manifest as the 4-year-old becoming annoyed or the 2-year-old being easily distracted.
  • Seeking Reassurance: The consistent FaceTime calls, even if met with resistance, serve as a crucial form of reassurance for the children and their father. The parent receiving the calls might not realize the child’s resistance is a complex emotional response rather than a true rejection.

What the Parent Can Do:

  • Acknowledge and Validate: Continue to acknowledge their feelings without judgment. Reassure them it’s okay to miss their dad and it’s also okay to enjoy their time at home. Phrases like, “I know you miss Daddy, and it’s okay to feel that way. We can have fun here too!” can be helpful.
  • Don’t Force Interaction: If the children are resistant during calls, don’t force them to talk. Encourage a brief wave or “I love you” and let it be. Pushing them can create more anxiety and resistance.
  • Focus on Connection, Not Performance: For the parent on the receiving end, the goal isn’t a perfect conversation. It’s about maintaining a connection. If the child is distracted, simply being present and available is enough. Sometimes, having the parent present during the call can help the child feel more secure.
  • Maintain Routine: Consistency in visitation and communication routines can provide a sense of security for children navigating these complex family dynamics.

This behavior, while perplexing, is often a sign that children are trying to process a difficult situation. By providing a stable, loving environment and understanding these underlying emotions, parents can help their children navigate these challenges.
